# TBT-287 Low Pressure Alarms

## Why It Matters
Low Pressure Alarms matters because an alarm or trip is the system telling you something is wrong — investigating properly before resetting prevents a bigger failure.

## KEY HAZARDS
- Restarting equipment without establishing the cause of a trip
- ignoring or silencing alarms without investigation
- electrical hazards during fault investigation
- repeated trips masking a developing fault

## REQUIRED CONTROLS
- Investigate the cause of any alarm or trip before resetting
- follow the manufacturer's fault-finding procedure
- isolate electrically before investigating electrical trips
- log alarms and trips to identify recurring patterns

## EMPLOYEES MUST
- Establish the cause of a trip or alarm before resetting the equipment
- isolate before investigating any electrical fault
- record alarms/trips and actions taken
- escalate repeated or unexplained trips

## EMPLOYEES MUST NOT
- Reset a trip repeatedly without investigating the cause
- bypass or disable an alarm or protective device
- ignore an alarm because it has occurred before

## GOOD PRACTICE
- Keep a log of alarms and trips for trend analysis
- check manufacturer documentation for known fault codes
- brief the next shift/visit on any outstanding alarm history

## EMERGENCY / INCIDENT RESPONSE
- Isolate the equipment if a trip presents an immediate hazard
- evacuate for refrigerant or fire alarms until confirmed safe
- escalate to a supervisor or specialist if the cause cannot be established

## Supervisor Discussion
- What are the main hazards of Low Pressure Alarms on our jobs, and which of today's controls matters most on this site?
- Which of these controls is easiest to skip when we're under time pressure — and what's the cost if it goes wrong?
- What would make you stop the job and ask for help?
