# AIM Act: Leak Repair Requirements for Appliances Containing HFCs and Certain Substitutes (Fact Sheet)

Fact Sheet
American Innovation and Manufacturing Act: Leak Repair Requirements for Appliances Containing Hydrofluorocarbons and Certain Substitutes
Background
The American Innovation and Manufacturing (AIM) Act, enacted on December 27, 2020, authorizes the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) to address hydrofluorocarbons (HFCs) in three main ways:
(1) Phasing down HFC production and consumption through an allowance allocation program; (2) Maximizing reclamation and minimizing releases of HFCs from equipment and ensuring the
safety of technicians and consumers; and (3) Facilitating the transition to next-generation technologies by restricting the use of HFCs in the
sectors or subsectors in which they are used. This fact sheet concerns the second area � maximizing reclamation and minimizing releases of HFCs, and in particular, the requirements for leak repair of certain refrigerant-containing appliances.
In this fact sheet, EPA is providing information on the leak repair requirements established under the 2024 Emissions Reduction and Reclamation (ER&R) final rule published on October 11, 2024 (89 FR 82682). The rule establishes regulatory requirements to minimize the release of HFCs and certain substitutes from equipment (e.g., air conditioning and refrigeration appliances) and to maximize the amount of HFCs reclaimed.
Summary of Leak Repair Requirements1
Beginning January 1, 2026, owners or operators and certified technicians must comply with leak repair requirements for certain refrigerant-containing appliances with a charge size of 15 pounds or more of refrigerant that contains an HFC or certain HFC substitutes.2 The leak repair requirements and the associated timing for leak repair are outlined in Table 1. For further information on leak repair, refer to the provisions at 40 CFR 84.106 and the ER&R regulations at 40 CFR part 84, subpart C.
There are several important exceptions to the leak repair requirements. Refrigerant-containing appliances used in the residential and light commercial air conditioning and heat pumps subsector3 are not covered under these requirements. Appliances using a substitute for an HFC with an exchange value or a global warming potential (GWP)4 of 53 or below are not covered by the leak repair requirements.

1 This information is provided for informational purposes only and should not be relied on for compliance.
2 Under the ER&R rule, a substitute for a regulated substance (i.e., HFC) means a substance that itself does not contain an HFC or an ozone-depleting substance. Refer to 40 CFR 84.102.
3 The residential and light commercial air conditioning and heat pump subsector includes equipment for cooling air in individual rooms, single-family homes, and small commercial buildings. Note that chillers and certain variable refrigerant flow systems are covered under their own subsectors and not the residential and light commercial air conditioning subsector. Visit the Technology Transitions page at the Frequently Asked Questions webpage for more information.
4 The AIM Act assigns an exchange value to each regulated substance. The exchange values included in subsection (c) of the AIM Act are identical to the GWPs of these substances included in the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) Fourth Assessment Report (2007). The lowest exchange value assigned to a regulated substance in the AIM Act is 53.

Comparison to Leak Repair Requirements under Clean Air Act Section 608
Most aspects of the leak repair provisions in the 2024 ER&R final rule, promulgated under subsection (h) of the AIM Act, are similar to the leak repair provisions under the Clean Air Act (CAA) section 608 refrigerant management program, but there are some notable differences. Table 2 highlights some similarities and differences between these two programs. For full details on the leak repair provisions under CAA section 608, refer to 40 CFR 82.157. For full leak repair requirements under the 2024 ER&R rule, refer to 40 CFR 84.106.

Applicability for Leak Repair Provisions

Refrigerant-containing appliances with a full charge of 15 pounds or more of a refrigerant that contains an HFC or substitute for an HFC with a GWP greater than 53.

Appliances with a full charge of 50 pounds or more of any Class I (e.g., chlorofluorocarbons [CFCs]) or Class II (e.g., hydrochlorofluorocarbons [HCFCs]) ozone-depleting substance as a refrigerant or blend containing a Class I or Class II refrigerant.

Refrigerant-containing appliances in the residential and light commercial air conditioning and heat pumps subsector are exempt from the leak repair requirements.
The leak repair requirements also do not apply to appliances (as defined in the regulatory provisions under CAA section 608) containing solely an ozonedepleting substance as refrigerant.

There are no exemptions for appliances with a charge size of 50 pounds or more of an applicable refrigerant.
The leak repair requirements do not apply to appliances containing solely substitute refrigerants (e.g., HFCs).

Leak Rate Calculation

Owners and operators must conduct a leak rate calculation, using the annualizing method or the rolling average method, after adding refrigerant to an appliance, with certain limited exceptions.

Applicable Leak Rate Thresholds

30% � IPR 20% � Commercial refrigeration 10% � Comfort cooling, refrigerated transport, or other appliances not covered as IPR or commercial refrigeration

Timeline For Repair

Leaks that exceed the leak rate thresholds must be repaired within 30 days (120 days if an industrial process shutdown is required). Repairs only need to bring the leak rate below the applicable threshold.

Extensions may be available.

Initial verification tests must be conducted within the 30-day window for repair (120 days if an industrial process shutdown is required). A follow-up verification test must be completed within 10 days of the successful initial verification test or of the appliance returning to normal operating conditions.

Leak Inspections

Timing for leak inspections is dependent on the charge size and type of appliance. Leak inspections occur on an annual or quarterly basis for a defined period.

Automatic Leak Detection (ALD) Systems

Some owners or operators may be required to install and use ALD systems if their refrigerant-containing appliance meets certain criteria. ALD systems can be used as a compliance option instead of leak inspection requirements.

ALD systems are not required but can be used as a compliance option instead of leak inspection requirements.
